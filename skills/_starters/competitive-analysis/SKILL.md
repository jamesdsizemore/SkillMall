# Competitive Analysis (Porter's Five Forces)

Apply Porter's Five Forces to assess the structural attractiveness of an industry and identify the strategic pressures shaping competition. A high-force industry is unattractive; a low-force industry has structural advantages.

## When to use

- Evaluating whether to enter a new market
- Explaining why margins are under pressure in an existing market
- Identifying where competitive advantage is most defensible
- Preparing for a board presentation on market positioning

## The Five Forces

**Force 1 — Threat of New Entrants:** How easy is it for new players to enter? High when: low startup costs, no regulatory barriers, no switching costs, established players have no meaningful network effect or economies of scale.

**Force 2 — Supplier Power:** How much power do suppliers have over your costs? High when: few suppliers, switching is expensive, supplier's input is critical and hard to substitute.

**Force 3 — Buyer Power:** How much power do customers have over your pricing? High when: many alternatives, switching costs are low, buyers purchase in large volumes, products are undifferentiated.

**Force 4 — Threat of Substitutes:** How easily can customers switch to a different type of solution? Different product category, same job to be done. High when: substitutes are cheap, accessible, and improving.

**Force 5 — Competitive Rivalry:** How intense is competition within the industry? High when: many equally-sized players, slow growth, undifferentiated products, high fixed costs driving price wars.

## Industry attractiveness score

Rate each force 1-5 (1 = favorable/low pressure, 5 = unfavorable/high pressure). Sum the five scores. < 12 = structurally attractive, 13-18 = moderate, > 18 = structurally unattractive.

## Limitations

Five Forces is a static snapshot — it describes structure, not dynamics. It does not capture complementors, network effects well, or platform businesses well. Pair with SWOT or Jobs-to-be-Done for a complete view.
